## Modelling decisions (slice 1)

1. **A driver is switches, not a formula.** Each output of an H-bridge is two switches (to its supply, to GND), a relay is its coil and two contacts, a ULN2003 channel is one sink. Its inputs are read from the solved voltages, so a driver works from whatever drives them — a pin, a rail, another module — and with PWM it is solved in each on / off state, as the LEDs are (M3.3).
2. **The L298N's drop is its switches' resistance** (≈ 5 Ω each): on 5 V a TT motor gets ≈ 3.6 V (≈ 130 rpm, not 180). Its logic needs 4.5 V: from its 5V screw, or from its 12V screw through its regulator while the 5V-EN jumper is on — a simplification: the real regulator's output on 5 V in is lower; its 5V screw is not driven as an output here.
3. **A motor on a pin is damage** when the pin drives it with more than its safe current: the pin gives all it can (48 mA on the Pico W, 15 mA on the micro:bit), and a real one is hurt by that and by the motor's kick. The card of screen 25c (with *Show me how*) is M9's fault table, as screen 04's; until then the message is in the console.
4. **A stepper follows its coils**: a new pattern a half step or a full step from where its rotor is moves it (either way); a jump of more it can't follow. No speed limit yet (the patterns are read when the circuit is solved, so a program's writes between two steps are not each a step).
5. **A servo turns at 600°/s** (an SG90's 0.1 s per 60° at 4.8 V) and draws ≈ 200 mA while it turns, ≈ 10 mA at rest — so VBUS's budget is real (screen 25's status bar).

## Modelling decisions (slice 2)

1. **What a display shows is computed from what the program sent it**, at the level its driver talks: I²C transactions (the OLED, the LCD's PCF8574), SPI bytes and CS's edge (the MAX7219), CLK's and DIO's edges (the TM1637). So any driver works — the pack's, a student's own, raw `writeto()` — and a mistake in the bytes shows on the display as on the real one.
2. **The window gets a display's picture only when it changed** (each chip counts its writes); an OLED's frame is 1 KB of hex.
3. **The LCD's contrast knob is not modelled yet** (screen 26's inspector draws a Contrast slider): the letters are always readable on 5 V. A wrong address is modelled (the backpack set to 0x3F, the code saying 0x27: EIO).
4. **A display's I²C pull-ups are not modelled** (as the MPU6050's): an LCD on 5 V pulls SDA and SCL towards 5 V on a real board; the Pico W reads it, and the sim doesn't warn.

## Findings (slice 2)

1. **No resistor on a digit's COM burns its first segment** (screen 04's fault, *"digit1 (segment a): the LED burnt out — 48 mA through it"*), and the pin gets an over-current warning; screen 26c drew the pin as damaged. The engine follows the LED's 30 mA limit, which comes first.
2. **A digit's segment b was called "blue"** in a fault: the RGB LED's naming of its dies (`:r :g :b`) caught the digit's segments b and g. Faults now carry the part's type.
3. **The TM1637's lines floated** on the ESP32 before its driver made them outputs (a warning): the module's own 10 kΩ pull-ups were missing from the model.
4. **The simulated micro:bit had no `spi`**: added (and to the editor's completions), so the MAX7219 runs there.
5. In the e2e test, a wire from GP20 lay over GP21's pin, so the real mouse took the wire, not the pin: the test wires SCL first. A student meets the same; the pins' points could be drawn over the wires (later).
6. **The component tests reached their 15 s limit** under coverage beside the other projects (the canvas draws more
   parts now): their limit is 30 s. An M10 console test (*a file that runs for ever*) failed once under that load and
   passed alone and in the next runs — a timing wait to watch.
7. An e2e test of M4's window layout (*at 1440 × 900: no horizontal scroll*) failed once in a full run and passed in the
   next — a second timing to watch.
8. **The RC522's driver people use** (danjperron's `micropython-mfrc522`) builds each SPI byte with `b'%c' % v`;
   MicroPython 1.29 — with `MICROPY_FULL_CHECKS`, as its Pico W and ESP32 builds have it (py/objstr.c,
   `mp_print_char`) — encodes that as UTF-8, so a value from 0x80 is two bytes (0x88 → C2 88) and every register
   read goes wrong; the sim, a real MicroPython, did the same. And its ESP32 branch calls `SPI()` with no id. The pack
   ships MicroLab's own (`bytes([...])`, SoftSPI on the Pico W and the ESP32) with the same calls. **To check on the
   teacher's Pico W**: whether danjperron's driver really fails there on 1.29.
9. The simulated Pico W and ESP32 had no `os.uname()` (the RC522 driver asks it): now they answer as the real boards
   did in M10 (*sysname='rp2'*, *'esp32'*).
10. **Not yet as on the screens**: the LCD's Contrast slider (decision 3); the status bar's *I²C0 400 kHz: LCD1 at 0x27 · OLED1 at 0x3C* line; the fault card of screen 26c (*Show me how*) is M9's, as screen 04's.

## Modelling decisions (slice 3)

1. **A NeoPixel is read at the bitstream's level**: the bytes and the timing the pin sends (`machine.bitstream`), each bit by its high time — not neopixel.py's calls. So the firmware's own module runs unchanged, a student's own `bitstream()` works, and a wrong timing or byte order shows on the LEDs as on the real ones. The line's reset (low ≥ 50 µs) is taken as the end of each `bitstream()` call.
2. **Its current is its colours'**, set as a load at the volts of the board supply it is on (its constant-current drivers draw the same from 3.5 to 5 V), so a supply that saturates — VBUS gives at most ≈ 1 A here — doesn't change what it asks: the warning says what the NeoPixels ask (1.45 A), not what the saturated supply gives. On a supply that collapses below 3.0 V (12 at white on the micro:bit's own 3.3V) it browns out: dark until the next frame, as its chips forget their colours.
3. **Too much current is a warning, not a stop**: the board keeps running, as a Pico W on its buck-boost would; a real computer might turn its USB port off, which the sim doesn't.
4. **A stick of 16 or 30 is drawn at the stick's pitch** (22 a LED), not a 30-a-metre strip's 33 mm, so it fits on the canvas.

## Findings (slice 3)

1. **The micro:bit's `neopixel` errors are its own module's**: micropython-microbit-v2's neopixel.py is plain Python over `ws2812_write`, so an LED past the end is MicroPython's *IndexError* (bytearray), and a colour over 255 is not checked — (300, 0, 0) is kept as (44, 0, 0), the bytearray's low byte, as on the real V2; screen 27b's *ValueError: invalid colour* is the V1's C module's.
2. **The solve went round for ever** with a ring browning out the micro:bit's 3.3V: its LEDs were forgotten only when the window's picture was made, after the pins had asked the circuit again (lit → too low → off → enough → lit …). They are forgotten as the power is found too low now (decision 2).
3. **An unlit LED's dies are drawn as rects too**: the e2e test's selector for an LED's body found four; each LED's body has its class (`np-body`).
4. **The inspector isn't shown while running** (the canvas is the program's then), so screen 27's *Now: 2 lit · 15 mA* row is the LED's and the stick's tips and the status bar instead; the inspector says *up to 720 mA at full white*.
5. **Not yet as on the screens**: the warnings' cards and Problems list of screen 27c are M9's (the run's faults carry them meanwhile); the id is NEO1 (one kind for both shapes), not STRIP1 / RING1.

## Modelling decisions (slice 4)

1. **A sound is a pitch, a volume and a waveform at each moment**, not samples: CODAL's synthesizer is followed step by step (the frequency and volume each effect sets, at the sample it sets them), and the window plays that as an oscillator. A SoundEffect sounds as on the board; an AudioFrame stream is heard as each frame's pitch (how often it crosses the middle) and loudness — speech-like or recorded sound isn't reproduced.
2. **Noise has no oscillator**: it is played as a sawtooth at its frequency (as buzzy, not a hiss).
3. **set_volume is the speaker's**: pin 0 still gets its square wave at the note (a buzzer's loudness there is its own, from the circuit), as before.
4. **The randomness of a sound expression** (mysterious, …) is seeded from the clock each run, as varied as on the board; tests seed it.

## Findings (slice 4)

1. **`music.play(..., pin=None)` played on pin 0**: the sim took None as "the default pin". On the V2 the default is pin 0 and None is the speaker alone (modmusic.c, microbit_pinaudio.c); now as the firmware.
2. **`set_volume()` did nothing** in the sim (it was kept, never used): the speaker's volume follows it now.
3. **A shape's last step falls at the very end of its effect** in CODAL's loop, so a 4-step linear sweep from 500 to 2500 Hz is heard at 500, 1000 and 1500 Hz; and the log shape (a SoundEffect's default) overshoots its end frequency (log₁₀ 127 / 1.95 > 1) — as on the board.
4. **An error raised in a timer's callback** (*not an AudioFrame*, fetched on the audio timer) isn't the program's in the sim: it is kept and raised where the program waits for the sound (on the board, mp_sched_exception raises it wherever the program is).
5. **Not in slice 4** (the micro:bit's module still lacks them): `compass`, `uart` (slice 5), `run_every`, `panic`, `reset`; `speech`.

## Modelling decisions (slice 5)

1. **A UART is followed at the bit level only where it matters**: bytes between two ends of the same framing (within 2 %) go as they are, each at the end of its stop bit; another framing is read bit by bit off the line as the sender put it there. A frame's errors aren't reported (the UARTs keep the byte, as the RP2040 and the ESP32 do with MicroPython).
2. **The adapter shares its ground through USB**: the laptop powers it and the board, so its TX and RX work with no GND wire (as on a real desk); its own 5V and 3V3 are sources from its GND.
3. **Sending from a part's card goes at the moment it is sent**: up to 64 bytes a message (longer is cut).
4. **The HC-05's AT mode** (EN high at power-up, its commands at 38400) and its pairing name / PIN are not modelled; JDY-31 is the same to the program.
5. **The GPS's time is the laptop's UTC** from the run's start; its first sentences come a second after it gets power (a real one takes longer to find a fix from cold).
6. **SPI modes**: the parts on SPI (the MAX7219, the RC522) take mode 0 (and mode 3, the same edge); modes 1 and 2 shift their bits one place; bits other than 8 are passed on as given.

## Findings (slice 5)

1. **The real Pico W prints `SPI(0, baudrate=1000000, …)`** — the divider's result, exact because MicroPython runs clk_peri at 48 MHz (not the 125 MHz clk_sys): the sim computes it from that clock (10 MHz gives 8 MHz).
2. **On the micro:bit the console is the UART**: after `uart.init(tx=…, rx=…)`, `print()` goes out of TX too (to the HC-05 on screen 28b, which said "print() goes nowhere") — the sim diverts the console's output to the port; `uart.init()` with no pins brings it back to USB.
3. **`readline()` with the default `timeout=0` returns a line as far as it has come** (each byte must already be there): the GPS test and the examples give the UART a timeout (or wait a moment after `any()`), as people do on the boards.
4. **`led.read_digital()` on the micro:bit makes the pin an input**: the first `serial-led` example turned its LED off by asking it; the example keeps the LED's state in a variable.
5. **A module's serial lines were nothing electrically**, so the ESP32's unpulled GPIO 16 / 17 wired to them were floating: each part's TX is now driven high at rest and its RX pulled up (47 kΩ).
6. **A serial part's view wasn't remade when its power changed** (only when its bytes did): the phone card said *BT1 has no power* on a powered module — found in the real app; the views follow power too.
7. **The terminal card's baud row and send row overflowed** the card (a segmented control of five rates and the line endings): both are dropdowns.
8. In the e2e test the board's `decode()` of the garbage it got from the terminal at the wrong rate raised **UnicodeError** — as a real Pico W does; the test checks for it.
